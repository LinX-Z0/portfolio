Overseas

Top-down pixel survival fishing. Resource loop, quota, and a QTE on a small boat.

Role
Mechanics designer, gameplay programmer
Team
5 — Team IDK
Course
CSD1451, Year 1 Trimester 2
Duration

You skipper a small boat. A random quota and a day limit sit on the HUD. Fish to pay the quota. Manage health and supply so the hull does not starve. Hit a wreck for a supply dump. Miss the quota, or hit zero health, and the run is over.

I owned fishing, the QTE skill check, shipwrecks, the health/supply bars, and shop upgrades that rewrite those numbers. I also drew the player boat textures. Quota RNG and player movement were Mandy's. Monsters, rocks, water, and day/night sat with Ethan and Renfred. Menus and shop UI sat with Timothy.

Design intent

The run is two clocks. Quota (Mandy's generator) is how you fail the job. The body — my health and supply bars — is how you fail the boat. Fishing is the only way to pay the quota. Wrecks are the only reliable way to buy time on the health curve. If those two verbs feel the same, the loop is mush. They should not: one is greedy, one is survival, and every system below had to keep that split honest.

Fishing spots

Three spots exist at any time. Collide one, enter a fishing state, leave the overworld loop. Animated markers, so a spot is a thing you steer at rather than a silent trigger. A spot leaves the world two ways: you fish it, or it ages out on a random 10–20 second life. Either exit deletes that spot and immediately places a replacement elsewhere, so the count never drops below three.

The timer is the half that carries the pacing. Replace-on-catch alone only reshuffles the ocean when the player acts, which means anyone who parks beside a spot keeps it indefinitely and the map goes static. A random life makes the water move whether or not you engage, so a spot you can see but have not reached is a decision with a clock on it. Random rather than fixed, so the respawn beat cannot be counted and camped.

Authored in Fishing.

QTE

Fishing versus none is a state. In fishing, a marker sweeps a 360px bar and you have 10 seconds to land it inside the green window. Rarity is picked when the QTE inits, and it drives both numbers that matter: each tier halves the window and doubles the marker speed. Green, red, and white overlays show where you are in the ask.

TierWindowHeightSpeedTime in window
Common30%108px200px/s0.54s
Uncommon15%54px400px/s0.14s
Rare7.5%27px800px/s0.03s

Both multipliers apply at once, so the time the marker actually spends inside the window quarters per tier rather than halving — just over half a second on Common down to three hundredths on Rare, a sixteenth of the reaction time across two tiers. That is the whole point of tying rarity to the skill check. A rare fish is a different test, not the same bar with a bigger payout attached.

Three tiers ship in the fish data. Epic and Legendary already exist in the QTE code, so a later tier is a data change rather than new code.

Health and supply

Two bars, bottom-left, 11-frame sprite sheets. Supply drains at a flat 5 per second on the water and does not stop until it is empty. Health does not answer to time, it answers to supply:

SupplyHealth
80 and above+2.0 /s
75 to 80no change
75 and below−0.1 /s
50 and below−0.2 /s
25 and below−0.5 /s
empty−1.0 /s

Those thresholds are raw points, not a percentage of maximum, and that single choice does more work than anything else in the system. At the starting cap of 100 you sit in the regen band for four seconds after a top-up. At a bought-up cap of 400 you sit there for sixty-four. A bigger tank does not just delay starvation, it multiplies the time you spend healing, which makes the Supply upgrade a health upgrade the shop never advertises.

The gap between 75 and 80 is a dead band where health does nothing. It stops the bar flipping between regen and decay while supply hovers on the boundary — the HUD frame is health/10, and an icon that ticks up and down every second reads as broken rather than as tense.

That is the body clock. Quota can be unfair in the other corner; if these icons are unreadable, you cannot make the greedy-or-safe call the loop is built for.

Authored in Bar. I also drew the player boat textures on Mandy's movement body.

Shipwrecks

Wrecks reuse the fishing-spot population rule: three in the world at once, random placement, replaced the moment one is consumed. Two textures. Collide one for +50 supply, capped at 100. Wrecks hold no despawn timer, though — an unclaimed wreck waits, because it is the thing you divert toward when the health curve turns on you, and a resource that expires on its own is not something you can plan a detour around.

Wrecks are not a second fishing verb. They dump time back onto the health curve so you can keep fishing. If wrecks paid the quota, they would steal fishing's job. They do not.

Authored in shipwrecks.

Shop upgrades

Timothy owned the shop UI. I wired what a purchase actually does to the numbers the HUD reads. Four lines, split by what they touch: two raise a pool, two bend a rate.

UpgradeBase costTouchesPer levelFully bought
Health25Pool+20 × level to current and max400 max
Supply25Pool+20 × level to current and max400 max
Armor30RateIncoming damage −10%−50% damage
Efficiency50RateSupply drain −10%2.5/s drain

Health and Supply run an identical curve. Both start at 100, both add 20 × the new level to the current value and the maximum together, and both open at 25 with every subsequent buy costing 1.5× the last, truncated to an integer. There is no level cap.

The gain grows linearly and the price grows geometrically, so value per coin peaks on the second and third buy and falls away after that. With no hard cap, the price curve is the cap — the run stops you from over-investing instead of a number in a config doing it. The first buy is deliberately the worst rate in the table, which keeps the opening purchase a real decision rather than an obvious one.

Health doubles as a repair because it raises the current value alongside the maximum, so spending to survive tonight is a legal play and not only a long-term investment. Rates price above pools because a rate change is permanent pressure relief, where a bigger pool still empties at the same speed.

Playtests

CSD1451 user testing, 26 surveys. Testers liked the pixel art, controls, QTE, and upgrades. The complaints: 15 of 26 named unrealistic quota as the worst beat, night was too dark, HUD icons too small, the tutorial was missed, and there were too many objects in the water. As a team we tuned day length, quota, and night, and made the tutorial unavoidable.

What I changed, and why:

  • QTE rarity curve — 10% shrink per tier to a halving. The old curve ran 30%, 27%, 24.3%, or 108px, 97px, and 88px on a 360px bar. Testers could not tell the tiers apart, so rarity was only changing the payout. Both curves are geometric, so the fix was step size rather than curve shape: 0.9 per tier is not a difference you can feel mid-action.
  • Supply drain up to 5 per second, regen threshold up to 80. A full bar used to outlast anyone's attention, and healing was the resting state rather than a reward. Supply was scenery, not a clock. It also gave Efficiency a job: against a 5-per-second drain, maxing it saves two and a half points a second, which is what testers meant by upgrades that help you survive.
  • Shipwreck payout up to +50 supply. Once supply actually bit, wrecks did not return enough to justify the detour, so testers ignored them. At +50 a wreck buys ten seconds of drain.
  • Fishing spots and wrecks down to three each. The “too many objects” note. At the old counts one was always within reach, so the crossing never happened and the drain never bit.

My bars were the right systems but too quiet in the corner — the body clock testers needed for the greedy-or-safe call was the one they could not see.

Outcome and reflection

Overseas shipped at the end of the 14-week trimester and graded an A. The HUD readability the surveys flagged was fixed before the deadline, so the bars ended up doing the job they were built for.

What I would change is not in the tuning list above. Every entry in that list is a number I moved, and moving them was slower than it should have been. I had hardcoded values I did not expect to revisit, nothing was exposed for editing, and I had no way to watch the sim while it ran — so each retune meant a rebuild to see whether a guess was right. The habit I took out of this project is to expose tunables and stand up a debug view before a system needs tuning, because any value can become one you need to change the moment someone else plays the game, including the ones that looked settled when I typed them.

The other lesson was about building on someone else's code. We discussed implementation and ideation as a team and still finished with different understandings of the same system, which is how you end up calling a function that does almost, but not quite, what you assumed. Agreeing on an idea is not the same as sharing a model of it, and I now ask whoever wrote a thing before I build on top of it.

Both went straight into the next project. On Hellmaker I built the engine's debug layer and the ImGui console the team used to watch the engine while it ran — the tool I wanted here.
