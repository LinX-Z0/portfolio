Acquit

A single-player courtroom strategy card game

Acquit puts you in the role of a defence attorney on the O.J. Simpson trial. The goal is not to recite the case — it is to build a playable legal journey: gather a case file, lock in a strategy before you enter court, then spend that preparation arguing for the jury.

I was one of six designers (Caven, Mandy, Lin Xin, Isaiah, Yan Xin, Novelie). I worked on how the legal process maps onto cards, phases, and scoring — turning investigation, pre-trial, and trial into one loop instead of a pile of themed trivia.

Design Rationale

The brief asked for an adventure/journey, so the whole legal process had to be the journey. A single shared deck was simpler to set up, but cards from the wrong phase kept showing up at the wrong time. We split the decks: non-trial cards for investigation and pre-trial, arguments and tactics for the courtroom. Setup is slightly fussier. Each phase is much more readable.

Investigation is hand-building. You draw a case, then spend Investigation cards to peek, search, redraw, or filter for evidence versus arguments. At the end of that phase the extras go away, so the skill is not hoarding — it is deciding what is worth keeping.

Pre-trial is the commit. You pick one Motion with a real tradeoff. Motion to Suppress makes evidence stronger, but a failed evidence-backed argument hurts more. Motion to Dismiss boosts your argument rolls, then taxes you for every argument the other side still wins. Continuance is a redraw: safer, less swingy. That choice is the “I have a theory of the case” moment before dice start mattering.

Trial is where preparation meets uncertainty. An argument is flipped with a baseline difficulty that already leans prosecution or defence. You may play evidence and/or a tactic, then roll. Evidence is not generic +1: some cards only fit specific arguments, some work for either side, some have a worse chance but a bigger jury swing. Synergies (domestic violence history with the abuse argument, DNA with crime-scene arguments, flight with “erratic behaviour”) reward a coherent case instead of spraying every strong card.

Jury tokens start undecided and flip as arguments resolve. First to 12 wins; if nobody hits 12, it is a draw. Playtests showed raw win rates swinging hard (one set went 5–7, then 10–2, 10–2), so we kept iterating numbers, made unused leftover cards a score penalty, and treated successful cards as a plus to the final case. The player should feel they improved the odds, not that the dice were the game.

Systems

The card economy settled around 40 cards: 10 arguments, 15 evidence, 3 motions, 7 tactics, 5 investigation. Turn sequence in trial is: flip argument, play up to one evidence and/or one tactic, roll, resolve jury, discard. If the hand is empty you can still roll the argument naked — so running dry is a state, not a hard lock.

Tactics are the interrupt layer. Objection and Confidence reroll failures (with a worse penalty if you fail again). Cross-Examination lets you play two evidence cards in one round. Expert Witness replays a spent card. Surprise Witness can cancel an opposing win at the cost of a discard. Motions sit on the table as a global modifier for the whole trial, so the pre-trial pick is a rules overlay, not a one-shot.

Most of the “technical” work on this project was table systems: difficulty values per argument, evidence modifiers that add or subtract from those checks, and a scoring model that could survive playtest without collapsing into auto-win or coin-flip. The card list is the spec for that.

Key Features

  • Three-phase legal flow with split decks
  • Investigation hand-building, Motions as trial-wide modifiers
  • Arguments with baseline odds, evidence synergies, jury race to 12
