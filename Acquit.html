You play defence on the O.J. Simpson trial. Build a file, lock a theory of the case, then spend that preparation on arguments. The brief asked for an adventure; the legal process is the journey. The other side is the trial deck, not a second player.

I was Design Lead. Every gameplay system and mechanic on the table is mine — investigation, discovery, evidence, motions, tactics, the card list, and the numbers behind them. I ran balance passes after playtests with the team. Art was team. Research was mainly others; I helped a little. I contributed on the rulebook and on Figma / card layout, but that was not my seat.

Design intent

A courtroom fantasy fails if it becomes a dice game with legal flavour text. The dice are how arguments resolve; they are not the game. The game is whether the file you built in investigation and the motion you locked in pre-trial actually change the odds you face when a card flips. If a player who prepared carefully and a player who rolled naked feel the same at the table, the phases were scenery.

That is why the three phases are not three flavours of the same loop. Investigation is hand-building. Pre-trial is a single permanent rules overlay. Trial is spending that preparation against a deck that leans. Each phase asks a different question, and the answers stack rather than reset.

Split decks

A single shared deck was easier to set up. Wrong-phase cards kept showing up at the wrong time — an investigation tool during an argument, a motion after the trial had already begun. I split the decks: non-trial for investigation and pre-trial, arguments and tactics for court.

Setup is fussier. Each phase is readable. That is the trade I would defend: a minute of sorting against a whole game of cards that only make sense when they appear.

Investigation

Hand-building. Draw a case, then spend investigation cards to peek, search, redraw, or filter evidence versus arguments. Extras leave at the end of the phase: the skill is what you keep, not hoarding.

Filtering matters more than raw draw power. A hand full of strong evidence that does not fit the arguments you will face is worse than a thinner hand that coheres. Investigation is where you decide what theory of the case you are actually going to try.

One motion

Pre-trial is one Motion, with a real tradeoff. That pick sits on the table for the whole trial — a rules overlay, not a one-shot.

MotionWhat it buysWhat it costs
SuppressEvidence lands harder when it hitsA failed evidence-backed argument hurts more
DismissYour argument rolls improveTaxed for every argument the other side still wins
ContinuanceA redraw — safer hand, less swingNo permanent rules leverage into trial

Suppress and Dismiss are bets that your preparation will hold. Continuance is the admission that it will not. Leaving Continuance as the low-leverage option is deliberate: the safe pick should feel like opting out of the motion system, not like a free fourth path with no downside.

Trial

Flip an argument with a baseline difficulty that already leans one side. You may play evidence and/or a tactic, then roll. An empty hand can still roll naked — dry is a state, not a lock.

Evidence is not generic +1. Some cards only fit specific arguments; some swing the jury harder with worse odds. Synergies reward a coherent case instead of spraying every strong card. Tactics interrupt: reroll, extra evidence, replay, cancel.

Jury tokens start undecided. First to 12 wins; if nobody hits 12, a draw.

Playtests

Early scores swung hard — 5–7, then 10–2, 10–2. Players who prepared carefully still felt like the dice had decided the night. That is the failure mode the whole design was trying to avoid.

What I changed, and why:

  • Leftover unused cards became a score penalty; successful cards a plus. Preparation that never left the hand was invisible to the scoreboard. Taxing dead cards and rewarding cards that actually resolved made the jury race answer the question the phases asked: did you improve your odds, or did you just hold pretty paper?
  • Argument difficulty and evidence modifiers reworked until neither auto-win nor coin-flip survived a pass. A leaning baseline is fine; a baseline that ignores the file is not.

The dice stayed. The scoring had to teach that they were not the game.
