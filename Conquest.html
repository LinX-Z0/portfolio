Conquest

You command a prince and two knights on a numbered hex map. Pick one of four strongholds; that choice also decides where the assassin starts. Lose your party or run out of eight turns, and the game is over. The eight turns stand for a rite of passage that will not wait. Land the assassin walks through is lost, like the kingdom slipping away. Conquering land gives you supplies, but you only win by killing him.

I was Design Lead. Every board mechanic is mine: formations, movement and the assassin's pathing, combat, locations, zone effects, event cards, action points, and supplies. It was the same role as on Acquit, focused on gameplay systems. I wrote the design statement and made the demo video. Art was done by the team. I helped with the rulebook and print-and-play layout.

Design intent

The design statement sums up the brief: medieval problems require management solutions. A succession fight and a time-loop assassin are old story ideas. The player's tools (action points as time, supplies split three ways, formations as placement) are modern resource-management problems in a medieval setting.

This keeps the game from becoming a scripted boss fight. If the assassin were a set-piece with phases, the map would just be something you cross to reach him. Because he moves across the map and undoes your territory as he goes, every hex you conquer is both a source of supplies and a shorter path for him. Conquest works when expanding your territory and hunting the killer are the same job, done against a running clock.

Action points as time

Each turn opens with an event card that changes the whole board. Then come three phases (morning, evening, and night) with 10 action points (AP) each, tracked on a D20. Moving into a territory costs 1 AP. You spend AP to move and to conquer, and the land you hold is what gives you supplies.

Splitting the day into three separate budgets instead of one pool of thirty matters. A single pool would let you save the morning's points for a long march at night. Three separate budgets force you to plan your route within each part of the day, which feels closer to medieval travel than a video-game stamina bar. The assassin also moves faster at night, so the last ten points of the day are the riskiest ones to spend.

Supplies

Between turns you spend supplies on three things, and you cannot afford all three.

SpendBuysIf you starve it
HealSurvive the next interceptYou never live long enough to use the build
Level knightsCombat power on the figures you haveYou unlock formations, but your knights are too weak to use them
Unlock formationsPositional combat toolsYour knights are strong but have no formation to fight in

Levelling and formations make your party strong enough to kill him. Healing keeps you alive long enough to use them. Splitting supplies three ways is the same idea as the shared gold in Overseas: if an upgrade never competes with survival, it is not a real decision.

The assassin

He is a moving threat, not a scripted boss. He takes the shortest path to the prince, skips small nodes, and moves faster at night. Any land he walks through becomes unconquered, so your income shrinks just because he passed through.

In a fight he picks from three moves based on how many of your figures are in the hex. That keeps combat from being a fixed script: the right formation depends on whether you caught him alone or as a full party, which is a positioning choice you made turns earlier, not a card you flip in the moment.

ToolWhat it doesRole in the race against time
ForestsCosts a whole phase to enter or leaveTrades a full phase of AP for distance
MortarsOnce-per-turn map-wide attack, if heldDamage him without having to catch him
WatchtowersWorks like a mortar, but only within its regionPressure near the stronghold you picked
Formations / levelsCombat layer once you meetTurn supplies into a win in combat

Terrain slows him down as well as you. Formations and knight levels are what you bring when your paths finally cross.

Stronghold pick

There are four strongholds, and your pick also decides where the assassin starts. Choosing a stronghold for its land also decides how soon he starts taking that land back. There is no safe starting point, so your very first decision is already about positioning.

Playtests

Early playtests felt slow and confusing. Players spent the eight turns figuring out the board instead of hunting on it. People who had not worked on the design needed a teammate to explain the rulebook mid-game. That is the wrong kind of difficulty for a solo strategy game.

What I changed, and why:

  • Raised action points to speed up the pace. With only eight turns to kill him, a tight AP budget felt restrictive rather than tense: players never reached the intercepts or formation fights the systems were built for. More points per phase let players conquer land, reposition, and still have time left to hunt, so the assassin's land-taking and the supply choices actually came into play.
  • Removed actions that only worked at certain times of day. Earlier drafts locked some actions to morning, evening, or night. That sounded medieval on paper but played like homework: memorise which phase unlocks which action, then hope you saved the right budget. We switched to one shared set of actions for all three. Night still matters because the assassin moves faster then; the difference is in his speed, not a hidden menu of yours.
  • Rewrote rules that only the team understood. Wording that was clear in design meetings confused players outside the team: how the assassin moves, how he takes land back, and what each phase's budget allowed. I simplified those sections so first-time players could follow without a designer next to them. Confusing rules act like a balance problem, because they hide whether the systems actually work.
  • Kept the three budgets, but made them work the same way. Morning, evening, and night still split the day so you cannot save everything for a night march, but each part of the day uses the same actions, just with different danger from the assassin. The complexity stays in the map and the hunt, not in a changing list of actions.

The eight turns and the day structure stayed. What we removed was the friction that got in the way of the hunt.
