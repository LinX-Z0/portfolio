Conquest

A solo positional strategy game

"Medieval problems require management solutions."

You are the Prince, eight days from coronation, trapped in a time loop. Your younger brother has sent an assassin who can reset the cycle every time you survive. You travel the kingdom to win favour, conquer territories, and command two knights. If the assassin is still alive at the end of day eight, the loop starts again — and he is stronger than you.

Design Rationale

The fantasy is medieval succession. The verbs are modern management: budget time, budget supplies, place people. Action points are not a generic energy bar — they are the day passing. Spend them on movement and conquest, and you have fewer hours left before night. That is how “time loop” becomes a resource instead of a cutscene.

Supplies are a three-way sink. Conquest feeds the pool; upgrades, new formations, and healing empty it. You cannot max knights, unlock every formation, and stay topped up. The interesting turn is choosing which of those three you can afford to starve.

Knights are positional, not just stats. Where you leave them enables formations. Combat bonuses come from layout — extra damage or defence if the squad is standing in the right shape — so the map is a tactics board, not a score track you paint red. Levelling knights and unlocking formations is the Prince learning command as the eight-day window closes.

The assassin is always chasing. He does not play a scripted boss pattern; he picks from three reactive moves based on the situation (for example how many enemies are in the fight). That keeps the hunt from becoming a solved puzzle after one loop. Day structure reinforces the same idea: morning, evening, night, with AP limits in each phase, so a “perfect route” still has to respect medieval travel instead of teleporting across the board.

Systems

The core loop is a state machine of days and phases. Each day has three phases. AP refreshes in a controlled way and is spent on move/conquer. Supplies are gained from conquered land and spent on three mutually competing upgrades. Combat resolves with formation checks against the assassin’s chosen move.

The assassin AI is small on purpose: a reactive selector over three attacks, not a learning model. The design problem is readability — the player should be able to infer why he swung that way from the board state (numbers, positions), then counter next loop with placement and formation unlocks. Progression (knight levels, formation list) is the long-term answer to a hunter who resets the short-term board.

Failing day eight is a full reset of the loop, which makes early-game routing matter: what you learn about his behaviour and which upgrades you rush are the metagame across attempts.

Key Features

  • AP as time, supplies as a three-way upgrade/heal sink
  • Knight placement and formation bonuses
  • Reactive three-move assassin and a day/night travel clock
