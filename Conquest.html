Conquest

A hex-based positional strategy board game

"Medieval problems require management solutions."

You command a prince and two knights, each with unique traits. The board is a hex map of numbered territories. You have eight turns to reduce the assassin's health to zero. Fail that clock, or lose the prince or knights, and the game is over. Between those two clocks — eight turns versus a hunter walking the shortest path toward you — the actual verbs are management: action points, supplies, and which land is still yours when he walks through it.

Setup is already a strategic fork. You pick one of four strongholds for the prince. That choice also sets where the assassin starts, so opening position is a matchup, not flavour.

Design Rationale

The theme is the rules. A medieval crisis (an assassin in your kingdom) is solved with budgets and routing, not a single heroic swing. Each turn starts with an event card that applies a global effect, so the "kingdom" keeps changing under you. Then you play three phases — morning, phase two, phase three — with 10 action points each, tracked on a D20. Traversing a territory costs 1 AP. You spend that AP to move and to conquer, because conquered land is how you get supplies.

Supplies are the between-turn economy. Holding territories feeds the pool; spending it is a three-way split: recover health, level up knights, or unlock combat formations. You cannot do all three well. Levelling and formations are how the prince's retinue gets strong enough to actually kill the assassin. Healing is how you survive long enough to use them. Starving any one of those is the interesting turn.

The assassin is a pathing problem, not a scripted boss. He moves toward the prince on the shortest path and ignores small nodes, so the hex graph matters. In the third phase he speeds up. Anything he walks through is unconquered — your economy can be peeled off the map just by him travelling. Forests counter that with "enemy shrouded": he has to spend an entire phase to enter or leave, which can buy you a whole phase of AP. Mortars (if you hold them) are a once-per-turn map-wide attack. Watchtowers work like mortars but only for a region. Terrain is a toolkit for controlling his clock versus yours.

Victory is only assassin HP to zero inside eight turns. That keeps the game from becoming a painting-the-map contest. Conquest is fuel for the hunt, not the win condition.

Systems

Board: hexes with reference numbers. Units: prince + two knights with distinct traits. Turn loop: draw event (global) → three phases of 10 AP → between-turn supply spend. Movement cost is uniform (1 AP per territory), so the cost of a plan is path length plus whatever you spend on conquest along the way.

Assassin AI is readable on purpose: shortest path to the prince, skip small nodes, faster in phase three, unconquer on pass-through. You can look at the hexes and predict where he will be. Forests, mortars, and watchtowers are the player-side tools for breaking that path or taxing him while you set up a fight. Formations and knight levels are the combat layer once you actually intercept him.

Loss is two-channel: the eight-turn hard limit, or the party dying. That means a greedy rush that gets the prince killed is as much a loss as playing too safe and running out the clock.

Key Features

  • Hex map, four strongholds, assassin start tied to your opening pick
  • Event card, then three phases of 10 AP tracked on a D20
  • Assassin shortest-path hunter who unconquers land; forests, mortars, watchtowers as map tools
  • Supplies between turns: heal, level knights, unlock formations
